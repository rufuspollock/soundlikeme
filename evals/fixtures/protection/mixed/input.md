# What we learned running the pilot

In today's fast-paced digital landscape, every team says it wants to measure things. We actually did, for over 3 years, and I want to write down what we found before I forget it.

The pilot ran across four sites. Samples were collected at 14-day intervals and processed within 72 hours; two runs were discarded because the seal on the storage vial had failed. The weighted interval score was used to evaluate the forecasts, since it is proper and decomposes into calibration and sharpness. Coverage was assessed at the 50% and 90% nominal levels.

It's important to note that the headline number moved less than we expected. Furthermore, the sites that moved most were the ones with the least reliable baseline, which is exactly the pattern you would predict if most of the movement were noise. Nevertheless, something real is in there, and I think it is worth chasing.

The vendor's own summary put it rather differently:

> This transformative deployment stands as a testament to what is possible when partners delve into the intricate tapestry of operational excellence, unlocking the full potential of the modern data estate.

Let's unpack this. The reality is that they shipped what was in the contract, and clause 4.2 required them to retain all licence identifiers in every copy of the Licensed Materials without modification, which they did.

The one thing that surprised me – and this is the part I keep coming back to – is how much of the value was in the boring plumbing rather than the model. Studies show that most analytics projects fail. We did not fail, and the reason is not that we were clever. It is that someone spent five weeks on `ingest --validate-schema` before anyone drew a chart.

At the end of the day, you can't fix what you can't see. The implications are significant.

So what now? I don't have a clean answer, and I have never once managed to explain this to a procurement officer!
