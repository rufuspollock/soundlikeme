# Copyright is a Monopoly (and isn't like normal property)

The equation of 'intellectual property' with property proper — the kind you can drop on your foot — gets made constantly, and most insistently by the people arguing for more of it. It is wrong. Not wrong as a matter of emphasis or framing, but simply wrong, and the error matters because of what it conceals: copyright and patents confer monopolies, and ownership of an acre of land does not.

## How is it different?

'Real' property — an apple, a car, an acre of land — can only ever be used by one person at a time. Economists call such goods 'rival': your use and my use are substitutes, and when we are near capacity they are strictly so, in that either I have the thing or you do. Giving someone exclusive rights over a rival good therefore does no particular harm. Only one person was going to have it anyway, and trade can be relied on to move it to whoever values it most.[1] Property rights here produce an efficient outcome, at least in the simple case; in more complicated setups one would want to think about complementarities and transaction costs.

A copyright is different in kind, not degree. A copyright in a particular text confers control not over this or that copy of the book but over every instance of such a book. Which is the very definition of a monopoly: sole supplier of some good.

And it carries all the standard consequences of one. Prices rise above where they would otherwise have been, access falls below its efficient level — the level at which price equals the cost of reproduction — and the gap between the two is deadweight loss.

The cost can be a good deal worse than that where reuse is extensive, which in creative work it always is, since new work builds on old. There the monopoly inhibits not only access by readers but the making of the next thing.

So the difference between 'normal' property and 'intellectual property' is the difference between giving someone control of one apple — the one they bought — and giving them control of all apples. The first does no harm. The second does a great deal.

## Does that make copyright bad?

No, and this is where the argument usually goes astray. That copyright is a monopoly does not make it bad *per se*. We care a great deal about the incentive to create, and the copyright monopoly does help supply it.

So we may well be willing to tolerate the ex-post costs of the monopoly for the sake of the ex-ante benefits — for the work that gets made because someone expected to be paid for making it. But that is a trade-off, and a trade-off that gets steadily worse as the monopoly is extended in term or in scope. Which is a completely different situation from the one we are in with 'real' property, where extension is not even a meaningful idea.

Macaulay put it better than I am going to, opposing a term extension in the 1840s:

> "It is good that authors should be remunerated, and the least exceptionable way of remunerating them is by a monopoly. Yet monopoly is evil. For the sake of the good we must submit to the evil: but the evil ought not to last a day longer than is necessary for the purpose of securing the good."

Nobody has ever written that sentence about an acre of land.

## But what about substitutes?

Rights-holders tend to reply that copyright cannot be a monopoly because close substitutes exist — Helprin runs a version of this, in trying to distinguish expression from ideas.

In the strict sense this is simply false. A monopoly is control over all, or most, of a market in a particular good, and here the good is the copies of a given work. If I have a monopoly in apples, the fact that you can go and buy an orange instead does not stop it being a monopoly.

In a broader sense the point is fair. The proximity of substitutes plainly affects the demand curve a monopolist faces, and so the price they can charge; in the limiting case where substitutes are perfect the monopoly dissolves entirely.[2] This is the thought behind the usual copyright/patent distinction — the claim being that copyrighted works have much closer substitutes than patented inventions do. Whether that is actually true is unclear to me. What were the close substitutes for Harry Potter? And plenty of patents have competitors sitting right next to them.

But notice what the argument costs the people making it. A copyright still permits its owner to raise price above reproduction cost — if it did not there would be no point in having one, at least as far as the economic rights go. And the existence of close substitutes cuts both ways: it lessens the harm of the monopoly, but it lessens the benefit too, since the benefit just is the extra revenue the monopoly allows.

To put it most bluntly:

If copyright isn't acting like a monopoly then, while causing little harm, it's also not doing much good.

If substitutes are close enough that the holder can only lift price a very little above the cost of reproduction — close enough, that is, that we would say no monopoly exists — then the gain to the holder, which is the whole justification for the arrangement, must be small in exactly the same proportion.

## Colophon

I wrote the first version of this over three years ago and then, for reasons I no longer remember, failed to hit publish. It started as a response to an article by a Mr Helprin, who later worked the thesis up into a book. The arguments I have had since, particularly with people who want copyright extended, have only made me more convinced that the basic point needs establishing first: copyright is a monopoly, and it isn't property.
