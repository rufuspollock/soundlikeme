# Copyright Is a Monopoly, and Property Is Not

There is a habit of speech so common that it has stopped registering as an argument. We call copyrights and patents "intellectual property," and having called them property, we reason about them the way we reason about a house or a field or a bicycle. Owners own; trespassers trespass; theft is theft. The word does the work, and the work goes unexamined.

The equation is wrong. Copyright and patents confer monopolies. Ownership of a house does not. This is not a quibble about vocabulary. It is the difference between a system whose costs are essentially zero and one whose costs rise with every extension of its term and scope — and it explains why nearly everything people find confusing about copyright policy stops being confusing once you see it.

## Rival and non-rival goods

Start with an apple. If I eat it, you cannot. The same is true of a car, an acre of land, a hammer. Economists call these *rival* goods: one person's use excludes another's. Because they are rival, exclusive rights over them cost the world very little. If I own the apple and you want it more than I do, you can buy it from me, and trade will tend to move it to whoever values it most. My exclusive right does not keep the apple out of the world; it only settles who currently holds it.

(This is a crude first approximation. Complementarities between goods and the transaction costs of moving them around complicate the picture considerably. But it is close enough to be going on with.)

Now consider a copyrighted text. Copyright does not give its holder control over one copy of the book, the way I control one apple. It gives control over *every* copy — every copy that exists and every copy that could be made. The holder is not one supplier among many. The holder is the sole supplier of that good.

That is not an analogy to monopoly. It is the definition of monopoly.

## What follows from that

Once you have named the thing correctly, the standard consequences of monopoly follow, and they are not mysterious. A monopolist restricts supply and charges more than the efficient price. Access falls below the efficient level.

For a book or a song or a piece of software, the efficient price is the cost of reproduction — which, for a digital work, is very close to nothing. The gap between that and the price actually charged is deadweight loss: value that simply fails to exist, benefit to no one, not even to the rights-holder.

The damage is worse in fields where reuse is extensive, and reuse is extensive nearly everywhere that matters. New work is built out of old work. Every restriction on access is therefore also a restriction on creation. The monopoly does not merely tax the reader; it taxes the next writer.

The apple makes the point again. Controlling one apple is unremarkable. Controlling all the apples is a different kind of power, and we have a different word for it.

## The trade-off nobody has to make about land

None of this means copyright is bad. It means copyright is a trade-off, which property is not.

Creation has costs, and something must cover them. The monopoly supplies the incentive. What we are doing when we grant a copyright is buying ex-ante creation with ex-post monopoly costs — accepting a loss in access tomorrow in exchange for a work existing at all.

The crucial feature of that bargain is that it gets worse as the term lengthens and the scope widens. Extra decades of protection add almost nothing to the incentive to create — an author is not much moved by revenue seventy years after death — while adding the full monopoly cost for every one of those years. Real property has no such structure. Nobody argues that ownership of a house should expire because its social costs accumulate.

Macaulay saw this clearly when he opposed term extension in the 1840s:

> It is good that authors should be remunerated, and the least exceptionable way of remunerating them is by a monopoly. Yet monopoly is evil. For the sake of the good we must submit to the evil: but the evil ought not to last a day longer than is necessary for the purpose of securing the good.

Read that sentence and then try to imagine anyone writing it about a farm.

## But aren't there substitutes?

The standard reply from rights-holders is that copyright confers no monopoly because close substitutes exist. Mark Helprin has made a version of this argument, resting on the distinction between protected expression and unprotected ideas: the idea is free, only this particular expression is controlled, and other expressions of the same idea remain available.

Strictly, this is false. A monopoly is control of all or most of a market in a particular good. A monopolist in apples has a monopoly even though oranges exist. That other things are for sale does not undo it.

In a looser sense the point is real. The closer the available substitutes, the flatter the demand curve facing the holder, and the lower the price the holder can sustain. Perfect substitutes would dissolve the monopoly entirely. (Antitrust lawyers have spent decades arguing about exactly where to draw the boundaries of a market; the difficulty is genuine.)

This is also what supposedly distinguishes copyright from patents: copyrighted works are said to have closer substitutes than patented inventions. I doubt it. What was the close substitute for Harry Potter? Meanwhile a great many patents sit in crowded fields with several competing ways of doing the same job.

## Turning the argument around

Here is the part the substitutes argument tends to miss. Copyright must allow holders to price above the cost of reproduction — otherwise it does nothing at all, and there would be no reason to have it.

Substitutes reduce the harm of copyright. But they reduce the benefit in exactly the same proportion and by exactly the same mechanism. If close substitutes hold the price near the cost of reproduction, then the extra revenue flowing to the rights-holder is correspondingly small — and so is the incentive to create that the whole system exists to provide.

So the defence proves less than it appears to. If copyright is not behaving like a monopoly, it is doing little harm; but it is also doing little good. You cannot have the incentive without the monopoly. They are the same thing seen from two sides.

Which is why the first step in any serious argument about copyright is to stop calling it property.
