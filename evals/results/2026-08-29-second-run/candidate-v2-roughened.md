# Copyright is a Monopoly (and isn't like normal property)

The equation of 'intellectual property' with property proper is made constantly, and made most insistently by exactly the people who want more of it. It is wrong. Not wrong in the sense of being a slightly loose analogy that we could tighten up if pressed, but wrong in a way that produces serious misapprehension about what copyright and patents actually do, which is to confer monopolies – something ownership of an acre of land does not do at all.

## How is it different?

'Real' property – an apple, a car, an acre of land – can only ever be used by one person or entity at one time; in the economist's terminology these are 'rival' goods. Giving someone exclusive rights over them therefore does no harm: only one person can have the thing anyway, and via trade we can be reasonably confident it ends up with whoever values it most.[1] So here, creating property rights leads to an efficient outcome (at least in the simple case – in more complex setups we would need to think about complementarities, transaction costs and so on).

A copyright is a different animal. A copyright in, say, a particular text confers control not simply over this or that copy of the book containing the text but over every instance of such a book. This is the very essence of a monopoly: being sole supplier of some good!

And it has all the standard consequences of one. Prices rise relative to what they would otherwise have been, access falls relative to its efficient level – the level at which price equals the cost of reproduction – and in between the two sits the familiar "deadweight" loss.

Furthermore, and this is the part that gets underweighted, the cost of the monopoly is particularly serious where we have extensive reuse, i.e. where new work builds on old, as the monopoly then inhibits not only access by users but the creation of new work by anyone else.

The difference then between "normal" property and "intellectual property" is the difference between giving someone control of one apple (the apple they bought, say) and giving them control of all apples. The latter does significant harm. The former does not.

## Does that make it bad?

No – and here is where the argument usually goes off the rails, in both directions. That copyright is a monopoly does not make it bad *per se*. We are, after all, deeply concerned with the incentive to create, and the copyright monopoly does help supply that incentive.

We may therefore be quite willing to tolerate the ex-post costs of the monopoly in exchange for the ex-ante benefits it provides in bringing new work into existence. But this is fundamentally a trade-off, and a trade-off that gets *worse* as the monopoly is extended – which is a completely different situation from the one we are in with 'real' property, where "extending" the right isn't even a coherent thing to ask for.

Macaulay made the point rather better than I am going to, opposing a term extension in the 1840s:

> "It is good that authors should be remunerated, and the least exceptionable way of remunerating them is by a monopoly. Yet monopoly is evil. For the sake of the good we must submit to the evil: but the evil ought not to last a day longer than is necessary for the purpose of securing the good."

This is not something one would ever write about normal, 'real', property.

## Substitutes (or, what exactly is a monopoly)

Some people, particularly rights-holders, tend to argue that copyright isn't really a monopoly because of the existence of close substitutes (Helprin does a version of this, where he tries to distinguish expression from ideas). In the strict sense this is simply false: a monopoly is control over all, or most, of a market in a particular good – in this case the copies of a given work – and if entity X has a monopoly in apples, the fact that I can go and buy an orange instead does not change the fact that X has a monopoly.

However, in a broader sense the point is correct, and worth conceding properly rather than waving away. The "proximity" of substitutes will clearly affect the demand curve a monopolist faces and hence the price they can charge (in the extreme case where substitutes are perfect the 'monopoly' of course disappears entirely).[2] This is the thought lying behind the usual copyright/patent distinction – the argument being that copyrighted works have much closer substitutes than patents do. Whether that is actually true is unclear to me: what substitutes were there for Harry Potter? And a great many patents have relatively close competitors sitting alongside them.

Nevertheless the fact remains that a copyright still acts like a monopoly, in that it permits the owner to raise price above what it would otherwise have been – if it did not there would be no point in having one, at least as regards the "economic" rights. And one has to be careful with one's logic here, because the existence of close substitutes may lessen the harm of the copyright monopoly but it also, and by precisely the same mechanism, reduces the benefits, which are just the revenue incentives.

To put it most bluntly:

If copyright isn't acting like a monopoly then, while causing little harm, it's also not doing much good.

Specifically: if substitutes are sufficiently close that the copyright holder can only raise prices to a very small degree above reproduction cost (close enough, that is, that we would say no monopoly exists), then the benefits of that copyright, in terms of increased revenue to the holder, must be small in commensurate degree.

## Colophon

I wrote the original version of this post over 3 years ago but failed to hit publish for reasons I no longer recall. Its creation was motivated by being pointed at an article by a Mr Helprin (who later fleshed the thesis out into a book). Discussions over the intervening years, especially with those advocating the extension of copyright, have only made it clearer to me how important it is to establish the basic point first: copyright is a monopoly and isn't property.
