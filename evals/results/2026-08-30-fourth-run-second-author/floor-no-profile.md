# Why Meanness Stopped Working

Here is something I've noticed and can't stop noticing. Among the most successful people I know personally, hardly any are mean. There are exceptions. But they are rare enough that when I go looking for them I have to think, and the ones I come up with feel like curiosities rather than the rule.

This is strange, because meanness itself is not rare at all. If you doubt that, look at the internet. A few decades ago, the only people whose opinions you encountered were famous people and professional writers — a small population, selected and edited. Now anyone can publish anything, and what has been revealed is not that people became meaner but that a great deal of meanness was always there and simply had no way of reaching you. The supply is enormous. So we have a puzzle: plenty of mean people out in the world, and next to none among the successful people I actually know.

Before going further I should say plainly how narrow my sample is. The people I know are startup founders, programmers, and professors. That's it. I have no idea whether the most successful hedge fund managers are mean; I don't know enough of them to say. I'd guess the most successful drug lords are mean, and I don't feel I need a sample to be confident about that one. So this is not a claim about all of human life. It's a claim about some large and, I think, growing part of it. Big regions of the world are not ruled by mean people, and those regions are getting bigger.

The best witness I have on this is my wife, Jessica. She cofounded Y Combinator, and she reads character with an accuracy I've never seen matched. Being near her when she's forming a judgment about someone is like standing next to a baggage scanner at an airport: things go in looking like ordinary opaque objects and come out with their contents displayed. She didn't arrive at startups from anywhere idealistic, either — she came from investment banking. And what she has found, over and over, is that the founders who succeed turn out to be good people, and that the bad ones fail. Not always, not as a law, but as a tendency strong enough to bet on.

So why would that be? I can think of three reasons, and I think all three are true at once.

The first is that being mean makes you stupid. That's the part I feel most personally, because I dislike fights, and I've spent some time working out why. The reason isn't squeamishness. It's that fights aren't general enough. Whether you win a fight depends almost entirely on the specifics: this situation, these people, this moment. So fights are won by tricks — moves that work in exactly one case and nowhere else. Meanwhile they cost as much effort as working on a real problem would. If you care about what your mind gets used for, that's an unbearable trade: the same fuel burned, and nothing produced that outlives the occasion. It's the feeling of wheels spinning at tremendous speed while the vehicle doesn't move. Startups don't win by stopping to fight. They win by racing ahead.

The second reason is hiring. A mean founder can hire people — there are always people who need a job badly enough to put up with someone. What a mean founder can't do is hire the best people, because the best people have options, and one of their options is not working for you. To get them anyway you'd have to be so extraordinarily persuasive in some other dimension that it overcame the obvious. Good people matter in any organization. In a startup, where there are so few of them and each one is such a large fraction of the whole, they're decisive.

The third reason is the one I'd have believed least when I was younger. Wanting to make the world better is an advantage in building things. The evidence is what happens at the moment of temptation. Nearly every successful startup gets an acquisition offer large enough to make its founders rich, and the founders who are in it for the money take it — that's what being in it for the money means. The ones who keep going are propelled by something else, and that something else is usually some version of wanting to fix the thing they set out to fix. They rarely say so out loud; it sounds sanctimonious, and founders are allergic to sounding sanctimonious. But it's there, and the richest founders are disproportionately drawn from their ranks. Which makes benevolence, of all things, a competitive advantage.[^1] [^2]

I don't think startups are a special case here. I think they're an early case — a place where you can already see clearly what work in general is turning into.

For most of history, success meant control of scarce resources, and scarce resources were obtained by taking them from whoever had them. Sometimes that was literal: pastoral nomads pushing hunter-gatherers off good land and onto the margins. Sometimes it was metaphorical, though only just — Gilded Age financiers maneuvering against each other to assemble railroad monopolies. Either way the game was zero-sum, and in a zero-sum game meanness isn't a handicap. It's a feature. Ruthlessness is precisely the trait that pays when what you want must come out of someone else's pile.

What's changing is that the games that matter most are increasingly not zero-sum. Winning now tends to mean having a new idea and making a new thing, and the person who has the new idea does not thereby take anything from anyone.[^3]

Games won by having new ideas are not new, of course. Archimedes was playing one in the third century BC. But notice how he died: killed by an invading Roman army. That is the whole mechanism in one image. New ideas require a certain amount of civil order to be worth having. Not merely the absence of armies — also the absence of economic violence, the kind nineteenth-century magnates practised on one another and the kind communist countries practised on their own citizens. What has to be true is that people believe what they make cannot simply be taken away from them.[^4] [^5]

Thinkers have always lived closest to that condition, which is why the trend started with them. When you look for people in the past who succeeded without ruthlessness, you find mathematicians, writers, artists. Their way of winning has been slowly leaking out of intellectual work and into the world at large, and as it spreads it inverts the old relationship between meanness and success.

I found this worth working out, and not only as an argument. Jessica and I are raising children, and we teach them not to be mean. Our house tolerates a lot: noise, mess, junk food. It doesn't tolerate that. Now I have one more reason to hold the line, and one more thing to say while holding it — that meanness isn't just wrong, it's a way of losing.

---

[^1]: Not every founder who accepts an acquisition offer is money-motivated; the claim runs only in the other direction, about the ones who decline. And a money motive can itself sit on top of a benevolent one — supporting a family, or buying the freedom to spend the rest of your life on things that don't pay.

[^2]: Not every successful startup makes the world better. But its founders believe it does, in roughly the way parents believe it of their children. The belief is blind and also completely sincere.

[^3]: Peter Thiel would object that successful founders still get rich from monopolies. That's largely true — but they're monopolies the founders create rather than ones they capture, and that distinction still implies a change in which sort of person wins.

[^4]: The Romans didn't mean to kill Archimedes. The commander had given orders that he be spared, and he was killed in the confusion anyway, which is its own kind of lesson about relying on the goodwill of an invading army.

[^5]: In sufficiently disordered times even thinking requires control of scarce resources, because staying alive is one.
