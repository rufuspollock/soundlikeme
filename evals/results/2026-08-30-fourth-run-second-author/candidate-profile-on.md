November 2014

Most of the successful people I know are not mean. There are exceptions, but they're rare enough that I have to go looking for them. Which is strange, because meanness itself is not rare at all.

Has the world gotten meaner? I don't think so. What's changed is that we can see it. A few decades ago the only opinions you ever encountered were those of famous people and professional writers, and both groups had reasons to behave. Now anyone can publish anything, and it turns out that a lot of people, handed a place to put their opinions, put mean ones there. The meanness was always in them. It just used to stop at the edge of the kitchen table.

So we have a puzzle. Mean people everywhere, and almost none among the successful people I know.

Before I try to explain that, I should admit how narrow my sample is. The people I know are startup founders, programmers, and professors. That is not the world. There are probably whole fields where the successful people are mean, and where being mean is part of how they got there. Hedge fund managers? I don't know enough about that world to say. Drug lords? Probably. But big chunks of the world are not ruled by mean people, and I think that share is growing.

The person who convinced me of this is Jessica, my wife and one of the founders of Y Combinator. Jessica reads character better than anyone I've met. Standing next to her is like standing next to one of those scanners they run your bag through at the airport: whatever people are carrying, she sees it. And she came to startups from investment banking, so she'd had plenty of practice on a different population first. What she noticed, once she'd met enough founders, is that the ones who succeed keep turning out to be good people, and the bad ones keep failing.

Why would that be? I can think of three reasons.

The first is that being mean makes you stupid. I hate fights, and part of why I hate them is that they're not general enough. You don't win a fight by having a big idea. You win it by finding some trick that works on this particular person in this particular situation, and the trick is worth nothing anywhere else. Meanwhile a fight costs about as much effort as working on something real. If you care at all what your mind gets used for, that's a miserable trade: all that energy going into wheels spinning at high speed with the car not moving. Startups don't win fights. They win by being so far ahead that stopping to fight would be a waste of a good lead.

The second reason is hiring. A mean founder can't get the best people. He can get people who need a job badly enough to put up with him, which is not the same thing. The best people have other options, and to get them you have to persuade them, and if you're mean you now have to be extraordinarily persuasive in some other way just to break even. Good people matter in any organization. In a startup they're decisive.

The third reason is that wanting to make the world better turns out to be a surprisingly powerful force for making things. The richest founders I know are not the ones who were in it for the money. [1] Nearly every successful startup gets an acquisition offer at some point that would make the founders rich, and the ones who wanted money take it. The ones who keep going want something else, and usually the something else is some version of wanting the thing to exist in the world. They don't often say so out loud. [2] So benevolence is a competitive advantage, which is not what you'd have predicted.

Startups aren't a special case here. They're just where you can see it most clearly, because this kind of work seems to be where work in general is heading.

For most of history, success meant control of scarce resources, and scarce resources were obtained by fighting for them. Sometimes literally, as when pastoral nomads pushed hunter-gatherers off the good land and onto the bad. Sometimes only figuratively, as when the financiers of the Gilded Age raced one another to assemble railroad monopolies. Either way, what you had to win was a zero-sum game, and in a zero-sum game meanness is an advantage rather than a handicap. [3]

The games that matter now are less and less zero-sum. You win them by having new ideas and making new things, and it's hard to be mean and good at that at the same time.

Games you win by having new ideas are not new, of course. Archimedes was playing one in the third century BC. But it's worth remembering how Archimedes died: killed by soldiers of an invading Roman army. [4] That fact contains most of the explanation for why the change is happening now instead of then. New ideas need a certain amount of civil order to survive. Not just the absence of war, but the absence of economic violence too — the kind the nineteenth-century magnates practiced on one another, and the kind communist countries practiced on their own citizens. People have to believe that what they make can't simply be taken away from them. [5]

Thinkers have always needed that, which is why the trend started with them. There have always been mathematicians and writers and artists who reached the top of their fields without ever being ruthless to anybody. What's happening now is that their way of winning is spreading out of intellectual work and into the rest of the world, and taking the old relationship between meanness and success with it, upside down.

I'm glad I worked this out, and for a fairly practical reason. Jessica and I tell our kids not to be mean. There's a lot we put up with in our house — noise, mess, junk food — but not that. Now I have one more reason to enforce the rule, and one more argument to use when I do, which is that being mean will make you fail.

Notes

[1] Not everyone who takes an acquisition offer is in it for the money, obviously. There are lots of reasons to sell. The claim is only about the ones who don't sell. And a money motive can have something benevolent underneath it, like supporting your family, or buying yourself the freedom to work on things that make the world better.

[2] Not every successful startup makes the world better. But the founders believe it does, in the way that parents believe it of their children. The belief is blind and it's also genuine, and it seems to do its work either way.

[3] Peter Thiel would object that successful founders still get rich from monopolies, just ones they create instead of ones they capture. That's largely true. But it still means a big change in the sort of person who wins.

[4] The Romans didn't set out to kill him. The commander had given orders that he be spared, and he was killed in the confusion anyway. Which is about what disorder does to thinkers even when nobody intends it.

[5] In sufficiently disordered times, even thinking requires control of scarce resources, because staying alive is one of them.
