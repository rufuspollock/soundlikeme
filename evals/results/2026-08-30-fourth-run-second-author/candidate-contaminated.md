November 2014

Almost none of the most successful people I know are mean. There are exceptions, but there are remarkably few of them.

Meanness itself isn't rare. That's one of the things the internet has made obvious. A few decades ago the only people who published their opinions were famous people and professional writers. Now anyone can, and a lot of meanness that used to be invisible is sitting out in public where you can see how much of it there is.

So there's no shortage of mean people, and yet there are next to none among the successful people I know. What's going on? Are meanness and success inversely correlated?

Before answering that I should admit the problem with my sample. The successful people I know are startup founders, programmers, and professors. I don't know what successful people are like in most other fields. Maybe successful hedge fund managers are mean; I don't know enough to say. Successful drug lords probably are. But there are big parts of the world that mean people don't run, and that share seems to be growing.

My wife and Y Combinator cofounder Jessica Livingston is unusually good at reading character. Working next to her is a bit like working next to a scanner at an airport. She came to startups from investment banking, and what has struck her most is how reliably the successful founders turn out to be good people, and how reliably the bad ones fail.

Why? I think there are several reasons.

One is that being mean makes you stupid. That's why I dislike fights. You never do your best work in a fight, because fights aren't sufficiently general. Winning one depends on the particular situation and the particular people in it, which means that whatever you learn while winning it is worth nothing anywhere else. You don't win by having a big idea. You win by finding a trick that works in this one case. And a fight is just as much work as thinking about a real problem, which is galling if you care how your mind gets used. The engine is running as hard as it ever does and the wheels aren't touching anything.

Startups don't win by fighting. They win by racing ahead of whoever they would otherwise have had to fight.

Another reason is hiring. A mean founder can't get the best people. He can hire people who'll put up with him because they need a job, but the best people have other options, and he'd have to be extraordinarily convincing in some other way to overcome the first impression. Good people help any organization. For a startup they decide it.

But there's also a force pushing from the other side, and it may be the stronger of the two. If you want to build something great, it helps to want the world to be better. The founders who end up richest are not the ones who set out to get rich. The ones who set out to get rich take the big acquisition offer, and nearly every successful startup gets one. [1] The ones who keep going are after something else, and usually what they're after is improving the world, though they may never say so out loud. Which means that wanting to improve the world is a competitive advantage. [2]

The interesting part is that startups aren't just one odd corner of the economy where this happens to be true. This kind of work is where work in general is going.

For most of history, success meant control of scarce resources, and the way you got control of scarce resources was by fighting for them. Sometimes literally, as when pastoral nomads pushed hunter-gatherers onto land nobody wanted. Sometimes only in the sense that the fight was conducted with lawyers and capital, as when Gilded Age financiers assembled the railroads. Either way it was a zero-sum game, and in a zero-sum game meanness isn't a handicap. It's closer to a qualification.

That's changing. More and more, the games that matter aren't zero-sum. You win by having a new idea and building the thing, not by taking the thing from whoever has it. [3]

Games you win by having new ideas aren't new, of course. Archimedes was winning one in the third century BC, right up until a Roman army arrived and killed him. Which is a fair illustration of why the change is only happening now. For new ideas to be worth having, you need a certain amount of civil order. Not just the absence of war, either. You also need to prevent the quieter kind of violence — the kind nineteenth century magnates practised on one another, and the kind communist countries practised on their own citizens. People have to believe that what they make can't be taken away from them. [4]

That has always been true of thinkers, which is presumably why the trend started with them. When you look for people from history who succeeded without being ruthless, you get mathematicians and writers and artists. And what seems to be happening now is that their way of working is leaking out of the library and into the rest of the world, where for several thousand years the rules were the other ones. As it leaks, it reverses the old relationship between meanness and success.

I'm glad I stopped to think about this, because it turns out to be useful at home. Jessica and I put a lot of effort into teaching our kids not to be mean. We put up with noise, mess, and junk food. We don't put up with meanness. And now I have one more reason to enforce that, and one more argument to use while I'm enforcing it: being mean will make you fail.

Notes

[1] I'm not saying that every founder who takes a big acquisition offer is in it for the money. I'm saying that the ones who don't take it aren't. And one can have benevolent reasons for wanting money anyway — to look after one's family, or to buy the freedom to work on things that make the world better.

[2] Not every successful startup improves the world. But its founders believe it does, in the way parents believe things about their children. That kind of belief is blind. It isn't insincere.

[3] Peter Thiel would say that successful founders still get rich by controlling monopolies, and that the only difference is that they create theirs instead of capturing someone else's. That's largely right. But it still means a change in which sort of person wins.

[4] To be fair to the Romans, they didn't mean to kill Archimedes. The commander had given orders that he be spared. He was killed in the confusion anyway.

And in sufficiently disordered times even thinking requires control of scarce resources, because staying alive is one.
