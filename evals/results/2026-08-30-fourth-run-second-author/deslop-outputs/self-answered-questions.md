Most of the people who are good at this are not the people who tried hardest to be good at it. That seems worth explaining.

Why? I think the answer is that trying hard to be good at something is a different activity from doing it. It puts you in the position of an examiner rather than a maker. You end up watching yourself, and you cannot watch yourself and see the problem at the same time.

Does that mean effort does not matter? Obviously not. The people I am describing work extremely hard. But they work hard at the problem, not at the project of being someone who is good at the problem. The difference sounds like hair-splitting and is not.

You can see this most clearly in kids. Ask a nine year old why they are building the thing they are building and you will usually get a blank look, because the question does not parse. They are building it because it is the thing in front of them. Nobody has yet explained to them that there is a career in it.

What happens to that? School, mostly. School introduces the idea that the work has an audience whose opinion is the point of the work. That is not a stupid idea - audiences do exist - but it is introduced about ten years too early, and it is very hard to get rid of afterwards.

Can you get rid of it? Some people manage. The ones I have watched manage it usually did it by accident, by getting so interested in something that they forgot to keep score.
