I have been going on about this for over 3 years now and I am no closer to being right about it, which is either a sign of persistence or a sign of something worse.

The claim is simple. When a public body publishes a dataset and calls it 'open', the word is doing an enormous amount of unearned work - it is standing in for a licence nobody has read, a format nobody can parse, and an update schedule nobody has promised. Furthermore, the people who use the word most confidently are usually the people furthest from the actual file.

Let me be clear: I am not saying that publishing data badly is worse than not publishing it at all. It isn't. A bad CSV on a bad portal is still a bad CSV I can download, and I would rather have it than a press release about it. Nevertheless, there is a real cost to the word 'open' being spent this cheaply, and the cost lands on exactly the people who cannot afford to hire someone to find out what the licence actually says.

The hard part - and this is the part I keep getting wrong - is that nobody involved is acting in bad faith. The data officer wants to publish. The lawyer wants to be careful. The procurement people want a supplier. Each of them is doing their job correctly and the result is a portal that satisfies everyone except the person who wanted the data. It is a wonderfully pure example of a collective action problem, and I have never once managed to explain it to a minister!

(Whether this is actually true of every portal is unclear to me: I have looked closely at maybe a dozen, which is not a sample.)

So what do we do? I do not have a clean answer, which is why this post has been sitting in my drafts folder. But I am fairly sure the answer is not another standard.
