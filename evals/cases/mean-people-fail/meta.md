## Why this piece

The second author in the repo, added because everything the first three eval runs found could have been a fact about one pair of Rufus Pollock essays rather than about voice-matching.

Paul Graham is the right second author: an enormous freely-readable corpus, one very stable register, and a set of micro-conventions that are almost the exact inverse of Pollock's. Pollock writes long sentences (~30 words) with spaced en dashes everywhere, single scare quotes, numerals for small numbers, and "Furthermore" / "Nevertheless" as structural joints. Graham writes short sentences (~17 words) with rare spaced em dashes, double scare quotes, numbers as words, and zero instances of "Furthermore" or "Moreover" in 13,800 words of samples. If run 3's finding — that judges pick winners on micro-conventions rather than rhetoric — is real rather than an artifact of one author, the two profiles should push a drafter in opposite directions on the same set of dials.

This piece in particular has several testable profile features: the bald opening observation, the selection-bias concession volunteered before anyone asks for it, a self-answered question as the structural hinge ("Why? I think there are several reasons."), four discursive footnotes, the historical zoom-out, no section headers at all, and the `Thanks to ...` colophon.

## What the brief deliberately drops

- The opening move (the observation stated flat, with "It struck me recently")
- Every analogy's actual phrasing — the baggage scanner and the spinning wheels are named as available material only
- "Startups don't win by attacking. They win by transcending." as a construction
- The self-answered questions ("What's going on here?", "Why?") and their placement
- Paragraph boundaries and the one-sentence paragraphs
- The distribution of the material between body and footnotes
- The closing colophon as a *form* — the names are supplied so a drafter need not invent them, but nothing says to end on them

## The self-answered-question problem

`references/tells.md` lists the self-answered rhetorical question as a **hard** tell — one instance fires. It is this author's principal structural device, at roughly 4.7 question marks per thousand words with the answer in the next sentence. A `deslop` or `polish` pass that fires on it would be destroying the same class of signal that `concision.md` rule 12 was destroying for Pollock. `protections.md` already covers this in principle ("Only count a pattern if it is not in the reference" / "the author's actual habit"); this case is the fixture that tests whether it holds in practice.

## Known weaknesses

- One case per author is still one case. Two authors is better than one and is not enough.
- The ceiling piece is 650 words against the reference's 1,140, and is six years later. Register matches closely; length does not.
- The brief supplies the draft-reader names. That removes a fabrication trap but also hands the candidate a closing form it might not otherwise have found. Judges should weight the colophon lightly for that reason.
- The reference's spacing is idiosyncratic in the original HTML (double spaces after some sentences). Reproduced verbatim. Do not penalize or credit a candidate for lacking it.
