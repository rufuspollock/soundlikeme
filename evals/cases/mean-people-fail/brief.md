# Brief: meanness and success

Write an essay making the argument below. Bullets only — supply your own structure, phrasing, and framing.

## Thesis

- Among the most successful people the author knows personally, very few are mean
- Exceptions exist but are rare
- Claim to be examined: meanness and success are inversely correlated, and increasingly so

## Setting up the puzzle

- Meanness itself is not rare
- Internet evidence: a few decades ago only famous people and professional writers published opinions; now anyone can; the volume of meanness that was previously invisible is now visible
- So: lots of mean people in general, next to none among the successful people the author knows

## Selection-bias caveat, stated before the argument

- Author's acquaintance limited to startup founders, programmers, professors
- Concedes successful people in other fields may well be mean
- Examples offered as concessions: hedge fund managers (author says he doesn't know enough), drug lords (author says likely)
- But: large parts of the world are not ruled by mean people, and that share is growing

## Corroborating witness

- Jessica Livingston — author's wife and Y Combinator cofounder
- Unusual accuracy at reading character
- Available image: proximity to a scanning device (airport baggage scanner)
- Came to startups from investment banking
- Her observation: successful founders consistently turn out to be good people; bad people consistently fail as founders

## Reason 1: meanness degrades thinking

- Being mean makes a person stupid
- Author dislikes fights
- Mechanism: fights are not sufficiently general — winning depends on the specific situation and the specific people
- Fights are won by tricks applicable to one case, not by big ideas
- Fighting costs as much effort as working on real problems
- Especially galling to someone who cares how their mind gets used
- Available image: wheels spinning at speed with no forward motion
- Startups win by racing ahead rather than stopping to fight

## Reason 2: hiring

- Mean founders cannot attract the best people
- They can hire people who tolerate them out of need for a job
- The best people have other options
- Persuading them requires being exceptionally persuasive otherwise
- Good people matter to any organization; for startups they are decisive

## Reason 3: benevolence as a force

- Wanting to improve the world helps in building things
- The richest founders are not the ones motivated by money
- Money-motivated founders accept the large acquisition offer that nearly every successful startup receives at some point
- Those who continue are motivated by something else, usually improving the world, often not stated openly
- Therefore a desire to improve the world is a competitive advantage
- Footnote material: not all founders who accept acquisition offers are money-motivated; the claim is only about those who decline. Benevolent motives can also underlie a money motive — supporting family, or buying freedom to work on world-improving projects
- Footnote material: not every successful startup improves the world, but its founders believe it does, the way parents believe it of their children; the belief is blind but genuine

## Widening the claim

- Startups are not an isolated case of the inverse correlation; this kind of work is where work in general is heading
- Historically, success meant control of scarce resources
- Scarce resources were obtained by fighting
- Literal example: pastoral nomads pushing hunter-gatherers onto marginal land
- Metaphorical example: Gilded Age financiers competing to assemble railroad monopolies
- Historically, success meant winning zero-sum games, where meanness was an advantage rather than a handicap
- Now: the games that matter are increasingly not zero-sum; winning comes from new ideas and new things
- Footnote material: Peter Thiel's objection — successful founders still get rich from monopolies, but ones they create rather than ones they capture. Largely true, and it still implies a change in which sort of person wins

## Why the change is happening now

- Games won by having new ideas are not new: Archimedes, third century BC
- Archimedes was killed by an invading Roman army
- That fact supplies the mechanism: new ideas require a degree of civil order
- Not only absence of war, but also prevention of economic violence — practised by nineteenth-century magnates against each other, and by communist countries against their own citizens
- Requirement: people must believe what they make cannot be taken from them
- Footnote material: the Romans did not intend to kill Archimedes; the commander ordered him spared and he died in the confusion anyway
- Footnote material: in sufficiently disordered times even thinking requires control of scarce resources, since staying alive is itself one
- Thinkers have always been in this position, which is why the trend started with them
- Historical figures who succeeded without ruthlessness: mathematicians, writers, artists
- Their method is spreading from intellectual work into the wider world, inverting the old relationship between meanness and success

## Close

- Author reports having found the exercise worthwhile
- He and Jessica teach their children not to be mean
- Household tolerances, stated as a contrast: noise, mess, junk food — but not meanness
- Result: an extra reason to enforce the rule, and an extra argument to use when enforcing it, namely that meanness causes failure
