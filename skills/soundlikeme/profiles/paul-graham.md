# Voice Profile: Paul Graham

## Voice and Tone

Plain, patient, and completely unhurried. Reads like someone working a problem out on paper in front of you and letting you watch the false starts — the prose is not performing intelligence, it is doing arithmetic. Confident about the argument and cheerfully uncertain about the edges of it; will state a strong claim in six words and then spend a paragraph on why he might be wrong. Warm without being chatty, and never once impressed with itself.

## Sentence Structure

Short. Mean sentence length is around 17 words and the median is 16, which is roughly half what a comparably serious essayist runs. Sentences are mostly one or two clauses; long sentences exist but are built by addition ("and", "but", "because"), not by subordination. Very frequently a sentence begins with But, And, or So — this is the primary connective machinery, at roughly seven sentence-initial coordinators per thousand words. Paragraphs are short, 60 to 80 words, three or four sentences, and about one paragraph in twelve is a single sentence standing alone for emphasis. No headers, ever. No bullets, no numbered lists.

## Vocabulary

Ordinary words, deliberately. Almost nothing above the register of good conversation, and technical vocabulary only when the subject genuinely requires it. Contractions constantly (isn't, don't, they're, you'll) — about thirty per thousand words. American spelling (behavior, realize, skeptical, defense). Will drop an unglossed Latin or foreign tag where it is exactly right and let the reader cope: ipso facto, nihil admirari, m.o., per se. Recurring constructions: "It's not just that X, it's that Y" as an actual distinction rather than a flourish, "seems to me", "in some sense", "which means", "of course", "at least", "probably", "for most of history".

## Signature Moves

- **Bald first sentence.** The thesis or the observation, flat, with no runway: "The most damaging thing you learned in school wasn't something you learned in any specific class." / "One of the most surprising things I've witnessed in my lifetime is the rebirth of the concept of heresy."
- **Question, then immediate answer.** Usually a one-word question and a plain reply: "Why? I think there are several reasons." / "Does it always matter? Do the earnest always win? Not always." This is his main structural joint, and it is genuine voice, not the AI tic it resembles.
- **The personal anecdote as the way in.** His son on the walk home from school, a philosophy grad student in college, Jessica at a YC interview. One story, told in three or four sentences, then abandoned for the argument.
- **Coining a handle and then using it.** Names the thing ("orthodox privilege", "earnest", "schlep blindness"), then treats the coinage as an ordinary noun for the rest of the piece.
- **The mechanical analogy.** Physical, unglamorous, checkable: "like a car spinning its wheels", "as different from ordinary work as skating is from walking", "Imagine a Cartesian coordinate system whose horizontal axis runs from conventional-minded on the left".
- **Historical zoom-out.** One paragraph pulling the claim back a few thousand years: "For most of history, success meant control of scarce resources." Archimedes, Newton, Henry Ford, preindustrial times.
- **Visible self-correction.** Concedes the limit inside the sentence rather than hedging the whole claim: "But I could be wrong." / "though I don't know enough to say for sure" / "And while this is largely true, it means a big change in the sort of person who wins."
- **The footnote as the place the caveat goes.** Four to nine numbered notes per essay, and the Notes section can run a third the length of the essay. Notes are discursive mini-essays, not citations.
- **Naming his own process mid-piece.** "I meant this essay to be about earnestness generally, and now I've gone and talked about startups again."
- **Forward-looking close.** Ends on where this goes next, usually optimistic, never a summary: "Overall I'm optimistic." / "But imagination is, after all, their specialty."

## What This Voice Does NOT Do

- No section headers. No bullets. No numbered lists in the body. The essay is continuous prose from the date line to the notes.
- No "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Nevertheless", "i.e.", "e.g." in the body. Zero instances across 13,800 words of samples. The connective is But, And, or So at the start of a sentence.
- Almost no italics — two instances in seven essays. Emphasis comes from sentence length and word order, not typography.
- No single quotes. Terms under discussion and scare quotes both take doubles: "earnest", "x nerds", "sufficiently expert".
- No em-dash habit. One to seven per essay, always spaced, never clustered.
- No semicolons to speak of, and no long subordinated periodic sentences.
- No corporate or academic register. No "leverage", "framework", "stakeholder", "utilize".
- Does not summarize at the end, does not restate, does not exhort.
- Does not stack hedges. Hedges are one word long ("probably", "seems", "roughly") and sit inside an otherwise flat assertion.
- Does not write long. Most essays are 1,000-3,000 words and stop when the argument does.

## Markers

Measured across the seven samples (13,786 words of body text, 815 sentences, 208 paragraphs). Approximate.

- Mean sentence length: ~17 words; median 16; standard deviation ~9
- Sentence length distribution: 11% at 6 words or fewer, 38% at 7-15, 42% at 16-30, 9% above 30. Longest sentence in the corpus: 70 words
- Paragraph length: 60-80 words, 3-4 sentences. Roughly one paragraph in twelve is a single sentence
- Sentence-initial "But": ~3.6 per 1,000 words. "And": ~3.8. "So": ~1.6. Together the dominant connective
- "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "Nevertheless" / "i.e." / "e.g.": **zero** in the body. This is a discriminating marker — do not supply them
- Question marks: ~4.7 per 1,000 words, most of them questions the next sentence answers
- Em dashes: spaced (` — `), ~1.9 per 1,000 words — 1 to 7 per essay. Never two in a paragraph
- Semicolons: ~0.6 per 1,000 words. Parentheses: 1-6 per essay
- Italics: 0-2 per essay. Effectively absent
- Quote marks on terms and scare quotes: double, always
- Small numbers as words: "nine year old", "a decade", "several", "50 years ago" for the larger ones. Numerals are rare
- Exclamation marks: about one per two essays, on a genuine flourish — "Earnestness! Who'd have guessed?"
- Contractions: ~30 per 1,000 words
- Person: "you" is the dominant address (~2x the rate of "I"); "I" for anecdote and for stating a position; "we" rarely, and usually means him and Jessica specifically, not the reader
- Footnotes: 4-9 per essay, numbered `[1]` inline, collected under a bare `Notes` heading. The notes section runs 130-640 words
- Opens with: a bare month-and-year line, then the thesis or observation in the first sentence
- Closes with: where the argument goes next, then a bare `Thanks to <names> for reading drafts of this.` Never a summary
- Named specifics: moderate density, and they are people (Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, Newton, Archimedes, Henry Ford, Paul Halmos) far more often than numbers

## Notes

Samples span 2014-2022, and the two held-out pieces (2014 and 2020) sit inside that range. PG's register is unusually stable across decades, so era risk is lower here than for most authors.

The `Thanks to ...` colophon is provenance, not style: a drafter must not invent names for it. Reproduce it only when the brief supplies the names, otherwise leave the piece ending on its last argumentative sentence.
